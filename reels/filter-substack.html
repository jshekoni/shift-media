This morning I worked out something about myself that I have been circling for years. There is a kind of shell around me. Things come in, a request, a message, an idea, a feeling, and instead of passing through me and out again as a response, they get caught. They become tangled in fears: of the consequences, of what people will think, of how I ought to respond and what I should do. And there they sit, while I try to strategise my way to an answer.

That, I realised, is where my overwhelm comes from. Its not that I have too much to do. It is that everything has to pass through a filter before I am allowed to act on it, and the filter is slow. When something arrives, I sift it and weigh it and test it against every possible reaction, rather than trusting my first instinct. Multiply that by every email, every decision and every opportunity in a day, and you have a permanent bottleneck. Without the filter, I think life would move through me freely, in and out, at its natural pace.

What the filter is made of

When I looked closely, the filter turned out to be built from a handful of fears. The largest was the fear of consequences. Beneath it sat the fear of being punished, the fear of being pushed out of the group, and the fear of being abandoned. Alongside those was a reluctance to be fully public, to let people see what I actually think.

I can see now that this filter has cost me. I believe it is part of the reason my acting career did not go as far as I wanted it to. When you are filtering everything that comes in and everything that goes out, there is very little of you left in the room.

The mannequin in the window

I see the extreme version of this in two women I know. From the outside, they look perfect. Everything they do is polished, because their filters are so strong that nothing unplanned ever gets through. But sitting next to them is like sitting next to a mannequin in a shop window. They are not quite alive. There is no charisma, no dynamism, and for all the polish, they rarely win.

There is a part of me that is like that, and I want it out. Which means I have to be a fucking firecracker. I have to be authentically myself and stop editing.

The world did not collapse

So today I decided to be unfiltered. Almost immediately I noticed the difference. I came across a post and, rather than scrolling past, I left three comments saying what I really thought. Before I did, the old filter ran its usual checks. This will upset people. The person who wrote this is powerful. What if it is used against me later? Every one of those thoughts would ordinarily have stopped me from saying what only I can see and what only I believe.

I said it anyway. I wrote the words, and the world did not collapse. Nobody arrested me. Nobody destroyed me. As I write this, something in my body feels as though it is untangling, a constriction releasing, and my voice is becoming my own again. I had not realised the block was a filter at all.

Where it came from

It is not difficult to see where it began. As a child I learned that being unfiltered had consequences: punishment, being pushed out, the threat of being abandoned. So I started to filter everything that came in and everything that went out, in order to control it and keep those consequences as small as possible.

What the work this morning showed me is that the consequences I fear now are probably not the real consequences. And when I went back into the past, I could see that even the consequences I feared then were probably not what I thought they were. I had no real idea, and I had stacked belief upon belief on top of that uncertainty.

So this morning I said it plainly to myself: I have no idea of the consequences of anything. This is far bigger and greater than me. For me, the strength to live that way comes from a belief that life is a magical masterpiece, of which I only ever see glimpses. If I want to take part in it fully and receive what it offers, the filter has to go.
